\section{Public Benchmarks}
\label{sec:public}

\subsection{Setup}

We test the failure mode and the cap on three public graphs with skewed positive supply: ogbl-collab and
ogbl-citation2~\cite{hu2020ogb}, and LiveJournal~\cite{leskovec2009lj}. Each graph uses a two-layer graph encoder with the same
two-tower InfoNCE loss and random negatives as production. Every run continues from a checkpoint trained with random
negatives for 24{,}770, 88{,}500 and 201{,}000 updates, mines for 12{,}385,
59{,}000 and 8{,}192 updates with the clustering refreshed every 500, 2{,}000 and 2{,}048 updates, and ends with 128
common random updates. Batches hold 476, 1{,}024 and 1{,}024 rows and target 79, 28 and 28 clusters, so the cap is 7, 37
and 37 rows per cluster. The five arms are random batching and clustered batching from fresh or cached embeddings, each
uncapped or capped. Each of three seeds has its own warm-start checkpoint.

We report two kinds of metric. The headline ranks each validation positive against the full candidate corpus (235{,}868,
2{,}927{,}963 and 3{,}997{,}962 nodes) with the other known positives masked, as recall@1000 and MRR. Next to it we report
each benchmark's official metric: Hits@50 on Collab's official negatives, and MRR against 1{,}000 sampled negatives on
Citation2 and LiveJournal.

\subsection{Uncapped mining collapses, capped mining does not}

Without the cap, clustered mining fails on every graph and every seed (Table~\ref{tab:public}). At the end of mining it
trails random training by 8.0 to 9.4 points of recall@1000 on Collab, 23.0 to 34.4 on Citation2 and 10.7 to 14.1 on
LiveJournal, and the official metrics fall with it, Collab's Hits@50 from 53.6 to 16.2. Its late batches are the
concentrated batches of Table~\ref{tab:concentration}: over the last 100 updates the uncapped scheduler averages 20 to 27
clusters per batch and reaches batches drawn from one or two clusters on all three graphs. The capped scheduler never draws fewer
than 34 clusters, and capped mining stays within 0.51 points of random at recall@1000 on every graph. Caching the
clustering signal does not change this picture: cached and fresh capped arms agree within a third of a point on every metric.

The cap does more than prevent the collapse at the head of the ranking. Capped mining raises full-corpus MRR over random
on LiveJournal by 0.5 and on Citation2 by 0.2, in every seed, and Collab's official Hits@50 by 1.0 to 1.2 points. On
Citation2 the official metric moves the other way: sampled MRR falls by 0.4 to 0.5 against random while full-corpus MRR
rises. The official protocol ranks the positive against 1{,}000 random papers, the full-corpus metric against all 2.9M.
One reading consistent with both is that training on regional negatives separates the positive from its close
competitors better and from random papers slightly worse, and only the second shows in the sampled metric. We therefore headline the full-corpus numbers on every
graph and report the official ones beside them.

\subsection{Recovery and query-level harm}

The collapse is recoverable. After the 128 common random updates, the uncapped arms trail random by 0.4 to 0.8 points on
Collab and LiveJournal and 3.0 to 3.6 on Citation2, and the capped arms end within 0.5 points of random. The cap's value
is therefore during and at the end of mining, where a production system would deploy the model, rather than a lasting
advantage over random training. Tracking individual validation rows through an earlier set of mining runs on the same three graphs
shows what the collapse is made of. At the end of uncapped mining, 14.6\%, 10.8\% and 8.8\% of Collab, Citation2 and
LiveJournal rows are top-10 hits for random training and misses for the mined model; with the cap, 2.5\%, 2.5\% and 1.9\% do. Of the rows that were hits before mining
and are lost under uncapped mining, 95\%, 84\% and 89\% are hits again after 512 random updates: the model forgets regions
it had learned, and relearns them.
