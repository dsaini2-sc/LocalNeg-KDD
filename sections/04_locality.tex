\section{Embeddings Already Know the Graph}
\label{sec:locality}

We begin with a model trained with random negatives only, the production default, and ask how its embedding space
relates to the graph it was trained on. The answer motivates everything that follows: the space is organised by graph
neighbourhood, so grouping users by embedding also groups them by graph region.

\subsection{Similar embeddings, nearby nodes}
\label{sec:locality:measure}

We sample 20{,}000 users from the evaluation panel and, for each, up to five targets at each exact graph distance of
one, two and three hops, five random users, and the user's own future friends. Distances are exact on the full graph:
two-hop targets share a neighbour with the user and are not adjacent to it; three-hop targets share none. We compare
the users with their targets by cosine similarity in the query embedding space, the space the clustering of
Section~\ref{sec:method} uses, for two random-negative models trained to three epochs from different seeds.

Similarity falls steadily with graph distance (Figure~\ref{fig:locality}). One-hop neighbours average a cosine of
0.704, two-hop targets 0.623, three-hop targets 0.446, and random users 0.072; the two seeds agree to within 0.001.
Graph walks favour well-connected nodes, so we also subtract, for every pair, the mean similarity of random users of
the same degree (log$_2$ bins): the gaps are $+0.63$, $+0.55$ and $+0.37$, so degree does not explain the effect. A
one-hop pair is more similar than a random pair 98.9\% of the time, a three-hop pair 92.9\% of the time.

The reference line in Figure~\ref{fig:locality} is the more telling number. A user's future friends, the positives the
model is trained to retrieve, average 0.605, below the user's existing one-hop neighbours. To the model, the user's
graph neighbourhood looks at least as much like a positive as the positives do. Negatives drawn from that neighbourhood
are therefore hard, and random negatives, 0.53 below the positives on average, are not.

\subsection{Clustering by embedding gathers neighbourhoods into a batch}
\label{sec:locality:batch}

A training example is a positive row, a user paired with one future friend; each user contributes one to four rows,
two on average. We measure what happens when 2{,}048-row batches are formed by clustering users on the
random-trained model's query embeddings, as the trainer does, rather than drawn at random. The measurement uses a
13.3M-node sample of the production graph and the trainer's own neighbourhood sampler.

Clustered batches bring whole regions of the graph together. The share of a batch's sampled one-hop nodes that are
reached from two or more batch roots doubles, from 2.2\% to 4.7\%. The users' positives come along: the share of
positives within one hop of \emph{another} anchor in the batch triples, from 0.9\% to 2.6\%, and the positives'
pairwise similarity doubles, from 0.017 to 0.033. The in-batch negatives each anchor sees are therefore drawn from its
own region more often than chance.

\subsection{Locality makes batches cheaper to encode}
\label{sec:locality:cost}

The encoder first projects every node of the batch's sampled subgraph and then runs one transformer sequence per root,
so the cost of a batch scales with its distinct sampled nodes and its distinct roots. Clustering cuts both. Because a
cluster keeps each user's rows together, a batch holds about half as many distinct anchors (2{,}012 to 1{,}022), and
roots fall by 24.6\%. Because clustered users share neighbours, their sampled neighbourhoods overlap, and sampled nodes
fall by 35.1\% per batch (441{,}461 to 286{,}525, medians). Grouping each user's rows accounts for 28.2 points of that
saving and neighbourhood overlap for the remaining 6.9. Each training step also encodes a random-negative subgraph that
clustering does not touch; per step, node-level work falls by 22.7\% and the number of sequences by 16.3\%.

The production trainers show the same effect. At full scale a random batch samples 917{,}794 nodes, a clustered batch
687{,}000 to 754{,}000, and the median time between batches drops from 4.40\,s to 3.60\,s, while the random-negative
subgraph is unchanged at about 53{,}500 nodes in every arm. Over a full run, clustered training steps are 12--15\%
faster than random ones (Section~\ref{sec:method:cost}).
