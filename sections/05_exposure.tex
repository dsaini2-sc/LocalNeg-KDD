\section{Exposure Negatives Hurt}
\label{sec:exposure}

\subsection{The conventional source}

An exposure negative is a user who was shown to the source as a friend suggestion during the label window and was not
added. Exposures are the standard industrial source of hard negatives~\cite{huang2020ebr}: they are free to log, they
come from the production candidate distribution, and a user who saw a suggestion and passed on it looks like a genuine
no. Our data preparation stores one exposure per source, the most-shown suggestion that was not added, 59.5M pairs in
total, and the historical trainer used them as extra candidates in the contrastive loss, next to the in-batch and random
negatives.

Adding the stored exposures back to an otherwise identical training run lowers macro recall@1000 from 34.09\% to
33.74\%, a relative loss of 1.0\%, and every cutoff and ranking metric falls with it (Table~\ref{tab:exposure}). An
earlier, independently initialised pair of runs measured the same loss, $-$0.346 points, and the production recipe had
already dropped exposure negatives after removing them gained 1.07\% relative recall@1000 on an older snapshot of the
graph (0.93\% for a variant that also dropped them before sampling). The harm sits where the exposures are: in a
1-in-1{,}000 sample of queries, those that own an exposure label lose 0.72 points in the add-back model and the others
gain 0.04.

\subsection{Which users, not how many}

Adding exposures changes two things at once: each batch gets more negatives, and those negatives are particular
users. The control separates them. It keeps every exposure slot, at the same positions and shared with the same
queries, and fills it with a uniform random user instead. The control matches the baseline at every cutoff, within
0.022 points, so extra negative pressure and the slot structure cost nothing. The whole loss, 0.357 points of
recall@1000, is carried by the identity of the exposed users.

The identity effect still bundles several properties of those users, and we do not separate them here. Some exposures
are not negatives at all: 0.51\% of stored exposure pairs coincide with requests accepted within the training window,
and another 0.51\% are accepted in the following seven days. Exposed users also come from the production candidate
distribution, so unlike the random substitutes they tend to be popular, recur across batches, and receive a different
frequency correction in the loss. What the control rules out is the explanation practitioners usually reach for, that
exposures hurt only because they add negatives.
