\begin{abstract}
Embedding-based friend retrieval on a billion-node social graph is trained contrastively, and its negatives decide both
what the model learns and what training costs. The usual sources of hard negatives fall short at this scale. Logged
exposures hurt: adding them lowers macro recall@1000 by 0.28 points. The harm comes from penalising each user against
its own suggestion, which is as close to it in the graph as its future friends; given to other users, the same
exposures help.
Mining with the current model would cost nearly four times random training. We develop clustered-anchor batching, which
starts from the model production already trains. Its embeddings, learned with random negatives only, are graph-local:
one-hop neighbours average a cosine similarity of 0.70 with a user, random users 0.07. Clustering anchors on these
embeddings yields hard in-batch negatives and batches that sample 35\% fewer nodes. We cache the clustering signal from
training itself. At full scale, cached clustered batching raises macro recall@10 by 18--19\% and recall@1000 by 5\%,
on 9--10\% fewer GPU-hours than random batching. In a ten-day online A/B test, the deployed model increased new friends
made with bidirectional communication by 2.09\%. Concentrated batches can also fail. On Collab, Citation2 and
LiveJournal, uncapped clustered batching reaches batches drawn from one or two clusters, and Collab Hits@50 falls from
53.6 to 16--19. A per-cluster row cap prevents the collapse in every seed and ends ahead of random training on each
benchmark's own metric. The concentration of the uncapped plan shows in advance whether a graph needs the cap.
\end{abstract}
