\section{Concentration Cuts Both Ways}
\label{sec:capping}

Clustered batching works because a batch concentrates on a few regions of the graph. Taken too far, the same
concentration becomes a failure mode. This section describes it, the cap that prevents it, and how to tell in advance
whether a graph is exposed.

\head{Regional forgetting} A clustering window hands the scheduler a set of clusters of similar anchors, each holding
the positive rows of its anchors. When positive supply is skewed, a few clusters hold far more rows than the rest. An
uncapped scheduler then spends whole batches, and runs of consecutive batches, on them. To isolate the effect of that
order, we ran controlled depletion experiments on Collab and Citation2. They hold the multiset of training rows fixed
and change only the order in which regions are consumed. Consuming a dominant region in concentrated runs improved that
region's own training loss in 46 of 48 imbalanced settings. It worsened the training and validation loss and
recall@10 of the other regions in all 48. The harm grows with the imbalance. Relative to a shuffled order, depletion
order costs $-$0.58, +0.34 and +1.88 points of mean recall@10 for balanced, moderate and severe supply on Collab, and
$-$0.03, +0.70 and +1.23 points on Citation2. The harm is acute rather than permanent: after 128 common random updates
the severe-order gaps shrink to 0.01 and 0.05 points.

\head{\rowcap{}, a per-cluster row cap} \rowcap{} bounds how many positive rows any one cluster may contribute to a batch. With a
batch of $B$ rows and a target of $k$ clusters per batch, a cluster contributes at most $\lceil B/k \rceil$ rows: 128
on the production graph ($B = 2{,}048$, $k = 16$). The scheduler fills each batch from the fullest clusters up to the
cap. A window whose largest cluster holds more rows than the cap can place in its batches cannot be scheduled exactly.
The scheduler refuses such a window rather than silently relaxing the cap (Appendix~\ref{app:results}).

\head{When the cap matters} What separates the graphs is the worst batch, not the average one
(Table~\ref{tab:concentration}). On all three public graphs the uncapped scheduler reaches batches drawn from one or
two clusters, and there the cap is worth 9 to 34 points of recall@1000. On the production graph no batch draws on fewer
than 16 clusters, and the same-cluster pair rate never exceeds 7.2\%. There the cap changes nothing measurable: capped
and uncapped runs differ by at most 0.26 points at every point of training (Table~\ref{tab:internal}). The reason is
upstream of training. Data preparation keeps at most four positives per user, so a 64-anchor cluster can hold at most
256 rows against a 2{,}048-row batch. The raw friending supply would not be so bounded: its 99th percentile is 82
accepted requests per user over 14 days, and its maximum 3{,}380. The statistic is cheap to compute from the
scheduler's own plan before any training, and it tells a practitioner whether the cap is needed. The cap costs nothing
where it is not needed, so the deployed model runs with it.
