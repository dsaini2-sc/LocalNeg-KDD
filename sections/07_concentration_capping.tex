\section{Concentration Cuts Both Ways}
\label{sec:capping}

Clustered batching works because a batch concentrates on a few regions of the graph. Taken too far, the same
concentration becomes a failure mode, and this section describes it, the cap that prevents it, and how to tell in
advance whether a graph is exposed.

\subsection{Regional forgetting}
\label{sec:capping:forgetting}

A clustering window hands the scheduler a set of clusters of similar anchors, each holding the positive rows of its
anchors. When positive supply is skewed, a few clusters hold far more rows than the rest, and an uncapped scheduler
spends whole batches, and then runs of consecutive batches, on them. To isolate the effect of that order we ran
controlled depletion experiments on Collab and Citation2 that hold the multiset of training rows fixed and change only
the order in which regions are consumed. Consuming a dominant region in concentrated runs improved that region's own
training loss in 46 of 48 imbalanced settings, and worsened the training and validation loss and recall@10 of the other
regions in all 48. The harm grows with the imbalance: the mean recall@10 cost of depletion order relative to a shuffled
order is $-$0.58, +0.34 and +1.88 points for balanced, moderate and severe supply on Collab, and $-$0.03, +0.70 and
+1.23 points on Citation2. It is acute rather than permanent: after 128 common random updates the severe-order gaps
shrink to 0.01 and 0.05 points.

\subsection{A per-cluster row cap}
\label{sec:capping:cap}

The cap bounds how many positive rows any one cluster may contribute to a batch. With a batch of $B$ rows and a target
of $k$ clusters per batch, a cluster contributes at most $\lceil B/k \rceil$ rows: 128 on the production graph
($B = 2{,}048$, $k = 16$). The scheduler fills each batch from the fullest clusters up to the cap. A window whose
largest cluster holds more rows than the cap can place in its batches cannot be scheduled exactly; the scheduler then
spreads that cluster evenly over the window and relaxes the cap only as far as needed.

What separates the graphs is the worst batch, not the average one (Table~\ref{tab:concentration}). On all three public
graphs the uncapped scheduler reaches batches drawn from one or two clusters, and there the cap is worth 9 to 34 points of
recall@1000. On the production graph no batch draws on fewer than 16 clusters and the same-cluster pair rate never exceeds 7.2\%,
and the cap changes nothing measurable: capped and uncapped runs differ by at most 0.2 points at every
point of training (Table~\ref{tab:internal}). The reason is upstream of training. Data preparation keeps at most four
positives per user, so a 64-anchor cluster can hold at most 256 rows against a 2{,}048-row batch; the raw friending
supply, with a 99th percentile of 82 accepted requests per user over 14 days and a maximum of 3{,}380, would not be so
bounded. The statistic is cheap to compute from the scheduler's own plan before any training, and it tells a practitioner
whether the cap is needed. Because the cap costs nothing where it is not needed, the deployed model runs with it.
