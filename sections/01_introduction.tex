% P1 -- Setting and why negatives matter.
Embedding-based retrieval is the first stage of friend recommendation at social-network
scale: a graph neural network embeds every user, and nearest-neighbour search over those
embeddings proposes the few thousand candidates that later stages rank
\cite{huang2020ebr,ying2018graph,johnson2019billion}.
\todo{One sentence on our graph: $\sim$1B nodes, $\sim$100M users with positive labels; model trained contrastively with in-batch and random negatives \cite{yi2019sampling}.}
The quality of such a model is set largely by the negatives it is trained against, and at
this scale so is its cost: every negative must be encoded from its sampled neighbourhood.

% P2 -- The conventional industry answer and its problem.
\todo{The two standard ways to get harder negatives. (a) Logged exposures, candidates shown but not added, are the conventional industry source \cite{huang2020ebr}. (b) Negatives mined from an approximate-nearest-neighbour index of the current model \cite{xiong2021ance} need periodic re-embedding of the whole corpus, which at a billion nodes is a job in itself.}
\todo{On our graph, exposure negatives hurt: adding them back lowers macro \recall{1000} by 0.35\pp{} (every ranking metric down), and removing them in an earlier production model gained 1.1\%. Why, in one clause, once the random-substitute control lands.}
\evidence{exposure-ablation/HANDOFF-EXPOSURE-NEGATIVES-2026-09-27.md}

% P3 -- The observation: embeddings already know the graph.
We start from an observation about a model trained with random negatives only: its
embedding space already follows the graph.
\todo{Nodes that are close in the graph are close in embedding space (Figure~\ref{fig:locality}): cosine similarity 0.70 at one hop, 0.45 at three, 0.07 for random users; a user's one-hop neighbours are even closer to the user than the user's future friends. Clustering anchors by embedding therefore puts graph neighbours in the same batch: anchors share 2$\times$ more one-hop neighbours and positives sit within one hop of another anchor 3$\times$ more often than in random batches.}
Two consequences follow. In-batch negatives drawn from the same region are harder than random
ones, and the batch's sampled subgraph shrinks because neighbourhoods overlap.
\todo{$-$35\% sampled nodes per batch; 12--15\% faster training steps at full scale.}
\evidence{embedding-locality-r1/READ.md; small-graph/batch-locality-r1/analysis/READ.md; full-scale-cached-mining/analysis-overhead/READ.md}

% P4 -- The method and the internal result.
We turn this locality into a training signal. Clustered-anchor batching groups the anchors of each training window by
their current query embedding and builds every batch from a few clusters, so each anchor's in-batch negatives come
from its own region of the graph. The clustering signal is cached from embeddings the training loop computes anyway
and planned on the CPU while the GPU trains. On the production graph, clustered batching raises macro \recall{1000}
from 34.8\% to 36.4--36.5\% after three epochs (+1.6 to +1.7 points, about 5\% relative), and the cached version
trains on 7\% fewer GPU-hours than random batching, including the one-time pass that fills the cache.
In a ten-day online A/B test, NGAME-style hard-negative mining trained without exposure negatives increased new
friends made with bidirectional communication by 2.09\%.

% P5 -- The catch: concentration cuts both ways.
The same concentration that makes clustered batches informative can also make them harmful.
\todo{When positive supply is skewed, a few dense clusters fill whole batches; the model fits those regions and forgets the rest. On three public graphs, uncapped clustered mining ends 10--34\pp{} below random at \recall{1000}, with some late batches drawn from a single cluster.}
\evidence{lab14-shared-cached-mining/analysis/READ.md; reports/COLLAB-READ.md; lab5 p14-p16 SCIENTIFIC-READ.md}

% P6 -- The fix and when it matters.
\todo{A per-cluster row cap bounds how much of any batch one cluster can take. It removes the collapse on all three public graphs, cuts query-level harm from 9--15\% of queries to about 2.5\%, and adds modest head-metric gains. A concentration statistic predicts when the cap matters: our production graph sits at the low end (largest cluster share 11\%), so capping is neutral there, and we run it enabled because it costs nothing.}
\evidence{lab12-query-tracking/README.md; capping-regime/READ.md; queue-audit/READ.md}
