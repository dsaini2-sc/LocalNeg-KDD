\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\head{Friend retrieval at scale} Embedding-based retrieval is the first stage of friend recommendation at
social-network scale. A graph neural network embeds every user from its sampled neighbourhood. Nearest-neighbour search
over those embeddings then proposes the few hundred to a thousand candidates that later stages rank~\cite{huang2020ebr,ying2018graph,johnson2019billion}.
Our graph has close to a billion users. The retrieval model is trained contrastively: each user is pulled towards the
friends it went on to add and pushed away from negatives~\cite{yi2019sampling}. The negatives decide much of what such
a model learns. At this scale they also decide what training costs, because every negative is a user whose
neighbourhood must be sampled and encoded.

\head{Challenges in negative mining} Industry practice offers two ways to make negatives harder than random users, and
neither works well on our graph. The first is logged exposures: suggested users who were not
added~\cite{huang2020ebr}. Exposures are hard. To a model trained with random negatives, a suggestion the user passed
over sits closer to the user than the user's own future friends do. Yet as negatives they hurt: adding them lowers macro
recall@1000 by 0.28 points. The same exposures cut both ways. As a negative only for the user it was shown to, an exposure
costs 0.49 points; reassigned as a negative for a different user, it gains 0.43, both against training without
exposure negatives. A user's own suggestion is as close to it in the graph as its future
friends, so penalising it teaches the model to discount graph proximity (Section~\ref{sec:exposure}).
The second way is to mine negatives with the current model~\cite{xiong2021ance}, which
means re-embedding candidates as the model changes. At our scale this is expensive. From our measured inference rate
and step times, refreshing an index of all users once per epoch and encoding one mined negative per training row would
take about 1{,}280 A100-hours. That is 80 hours on our 16 GPUs, nearly four times the 330 A100-hours of random training
(Appendix~\ref{app:ance}).

\head{Embeddings already know the graph} We start instead from a property of the model deployed in production. 
In an embedding space learned with random negatives only, embedding similarity follows the graph. A user's one-hop
neighbours average a cosine similarity of 0.70 with the user, three-hop users 0.45 and random users 0.07
(Figure~\ref{fig:locality}). The structure is learned: an untrained encoder barely shows it. 
A user's neighbours are even closer to the user than the friends the user will add next. This property has two consequences that we leverage for more accurate and faster training.
First, grouping users by embedding groups them by graph region, so each user's in-batch negatives come from its own
neighbourhood and are hard. Second, grouped users share neighbours, so the batch's sampled subgraph shrinks. Clustered
batches sample 35\% fewer nodes, and a clustered training step is 16\% faster at full scale
(Section~\ref{sec:locality}).

\head{Clustered-anchor batching} Using clustered anchors to create batches turns this property into a training signal;
we call the method \ours{}. Periodically, we cluster the anchors of the next window by their query embeddings and build
each batch from a few clusters. The embeddings come from a cache that the training loop fills as a by-product, and the
clustering runs on the CPU while the GPU trains. On the production graph, after three epochs, \ours{} raises macro recall
at 10, 100, 500 and 1000 from 2.71\%, 12.2\%, 26.7\% and 34.8\% to 3.20\%, 13.6\%, 28.4\% and 36.4\%. These are relative
gains of 18\%, 11\%, 6\% and 5\%. It trains on 9\% fewer GPU-hours than random batching, including the one-time pass that
fills the cache (Section~\ref{sec:method}). In an online A/B test on live app traffic, the deployed model, trained with
\ours{} and without exposure negatives, increased new friends made with bidirectional communication by 2.09\%.

\head{Concentration cuts both ways} The concentration that makes clustered batches informative can also make them
harmful. When a few clusters hold far more positive rows than the rest, the scheduler fills whole batches, and runs of
batches, from one region. The model then fits that region and forgets the others. On the public Collab, Citation2 and
LiveJournal benchmarks, clustered batching without a cap ends far below random training on each benchmark's standard
metric. Collab's Hits@50 falls from 53.6 to 18.8, and full-corpus MRR ($\times 100$) falls from 7.6 to 4.4 on Citation2
and from 15.2 to 11.5 on LiveJournal. Its worst late batches draw on one or two clusters (Section~\ref{sec:public}).
To prevent this, we introduce \rowcap{}, a per-cluster row cap within \ours{} that bounds the rows any one cluster
may contribute to a batch. \rowcap{} removes the collapse on all three graphs in every seed, and \ours{} ends ahead of random training: Hits@50 on Collab rises by 1.2 points, and full-corpus
MRR by 0.2 on Citation2 and 0.5 on LiveJournal. Whether a graph needs the cap can be read off before training, from how
concentrated its uncapped batches become. Our production graph sits at the safe end, because the dataset has
at most four positives per user. There the cap has little to bound and is neutral, and the deployed model runs with it
at no cost (Section~\ref{sec:capping}).

\head{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A measurement on a billion-node social graph: embeddings trained with random negatives are graph-local. This
        makes embedding-clustered batches both harder and cheaper to train on.
  \item Evidence that exposure negatives, the conventional industry source of hard negatives, hurt retrieval on this
        graph, and why: penalising each user against its own graph-close suggestion teaches the model to discount graph
        proximity. Given to other users, the same exposures gain 0.43 points of recall@1000.
  \item \ours{}: clustered-anchor batching with a cached clustering signal and the row cap below. Over random
        batching at full scale, it gains 18\% relative macro recall@10 and 5\% at recall@1000, at 9\% lower training
        cost.
  \item An online A/B test of the deployed model: +2.09\% new friends made with bidirectional communication and
        +1.01\% new friends made.
  \item A failure mode of clustered mining, regional forgetting under concentrated batches, and \rowcap{}, a
        per-cluster row cap that prevents it on three public graph benchmarks. A batch-concentration statistic shows in advance
        when the cap is needed.
\end{itemize}
