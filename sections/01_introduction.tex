\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\head{Friend retrieval at scale} Embedding-based retrieval is the first stage of friend recommendation at
social-network scale. A graph neural network embeds every user from its sampled neighbourhood. Nearest-neighbour search
over those embeddings then proposes the few hundred to a thousand candidates that later stages rank~\cite{huang2020ebr,ying2018graph,johnson2019billion}.
Our graph has close to a billion users. The retrieval model is trained contrastively: each user is pulled towards the
friends it went on to add and pushed away from negatives~\cite{yi2019sampling}. The negatives decide much of what such
a model learns. At this scale they also decide what training costs, because every negative is a user whose
neighbourhood must be sampled and encoded.

\head{Challenges in negative mining} Industry practice offers two ways to make negatives harder than random users, and
neither works well on our graph. The first is logged exposures: suggested users who were not
added~\cite{huang2020ebr}. Exposures are hard. To a model trained with random negatives, a suggestion the user passed
over sits closer to the user than the user's own future friends do. Yet as negatives they hurt: adding them lowers macro
recall@1000 by 0.28 points. The same exposures cut both ways. Used against the user they were shown to, they cost 0.49
points; given to other users, they gain 0.43. A user's own suggestion is as close to it in the graph as its future
friends, so penalising it teaches the model to discount graph proximity (Section~\ref{sec:exposure}).
The second way is to mine negatives with the current model~\cite{xiong2021ance}, which
means re-embedding candidates as the model changes. At our scale this is expensive. From our measured inference rate
and step times, refreshing an index of all users once per epoch and encoding one mined negative per training row would
take about 1{,}280 A100-hours. That is 80 hours on our 16 GPUs, nearly four times the 330 A100-hours of random training
(Appendix~\ref{app:ance}).

\head{Embeddings already know the graph} We start instead from a property of the model the production system already
trains. In an embedding space learned with random negatives only, similarity follows the graph. A user's one-hop
neighbours average a cosine similarity of 0.70 with the user, three-hop users 0.45 and random users 0.07
(Figure~\ref{fig:locality}). The structure is learned within the first 50 updates; an untrained encoder barely shows
it. A user's neighbours are even closer to the user than the friends the user will add next. Two consequences follow.
First, grouping users by embedding groups them by graph region, so each user's in-batch negatives come from its own
neighbourhood and are hard. Second, grouped users share neighbours, so the batch's sampled subgraph shrinks. Clustered
batches sample 35\% fewer nodes, and a clustered training step is 15--17\% faster at full scale
(Section~\ref{sec:locality}).

\head{Clustered-anchor batching} Clustered-anchor batching turns this property into a training signal. Every 50
updates, we cluster the anchors of the next window by their query embeddings and build each batch from a few clusters.
The embeddings come from a cache that the training loop fills as a by-product, and the clustering runs on the CPU while
the GPU trains. On the production graph, after three epochs, clustered batching raises macro recall at 10, 100, 500 and
1000 from 2.71\%, 12.2\%, 26.7\% and 34.8\% to 3.20--3.24\%, 13.6--13.7\%, 28.4--28.5\% and 36.4--36.5\%. These are
relative gains of 18--20\%, 11--12\%, 6--7\% and 5\%. The cached version trains on 9--10\% fewer GPU-hours than random
batching, including the one-time pass that fills the cache (Section~\ref{sec:method}). In a ten-day online A/B test,
the deployed model, trained with this mining and without exposure negatives, increased new friends made with
bidirectional communication by 2.09\%.

\head{Concentration cuts both ways} The concentration that makes clustered batches informative can also make them
harmful. When a few clusters hold far more positive rows than the rest, the scheduler fills whole batches, and runs of
batches, from one region. The model then fits that region and forgets the others. On Collab, Citation2 and
LiveJournal, uncapped clustered mining ends far below random training on each benchmark's own metric. Collab's Hits@50
falls from 53.6 to 16--19. Full-corpus MRR ($\times 100$) falls from 7.6 to 3.4--4.4 on Citation2 and from 15.2 to
11.6--12.4 on LiveJournal. Its worst late batches draw on one or two clusters (Section~\ref{sec:public}). A
per-cluster row cap bounds the rows any one cluster may contribute to a batch. It removes the collapse on all three
graphs in every seed, and capped mining ends ahead of random training. Hits@50 on Collab rises by 1.0--1.2 points, and
full-corpus MRR by 0.2 on Citation2 and 0.5 on LiveJournal. Whether a graph needs the cap shows in how concentrated
its uncapped batches become. Our production graph sits at the safe end, because data preparation keeps at most four
positives per user. There the cap is neutral, and the deployed model runs with it at no cost
(Section~\ref{sec:capping}).

\head{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A measurement on a billion-node social graph: embeddings trained with random negatives are graph-local. This
        makes embedding-clustered batches both harder and cheaper to train on.
  \item Evidence that exposure negatives, the conventional industry source of hard negatives, hurt retrieval on this
        graph, and why: penalising each user against its own graph-close suggestion teaches the model to discount graph
        proximity. Given to other users, the same exposures gain 0.43 points of recall@1000.
  \item Clustered-anchor mining with a cached clustering signal. Over random batching at full scale, it gains 18--19\%
        relative macro recall@10 and 5\% at recall@1000, at 9--10\% lower training cost.
  \item An online A/B test of the deployed model: +2.09\% new friends made with bidirectional communication and
        +1.01\% new friends made.
  \item A failure mode of clustered mining, regional forgetting under concentrated batches. A per-cluster row cap
        prevents it on three public graphs, and a batch-concentration statistic shows when the cap is needed.
\end{itemize}
