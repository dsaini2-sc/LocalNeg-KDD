\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\head{Negatives for embedding-based retrieval} Two-tower retrieval models are commonly trained with in-batch and
sampled negatives, corrected for sampling frequency with a logQ term~\cite{yi2019sampling}. Harder negatives come from
two places. The first is logged impressions. Facebook's embedding-based search found that training on non-click
impressions instead of random negatives cost 55\% absolute recall for people search. It attributes this to impressions
over-representing hard cases, and found that mined hard negatives help only when blended with random
ones~\cite{huang2020ebr}. The second is mining with the model itself. Dense text retrieval mines negatives from an
approximate-nearest-neighbour index that is periodically rebuilt~\cite{xiong2021ance,karpukhin2020dpr}, and has to
filter the false negatives such mining brings in~\cite{qu2021rocketqa}. PinSage draws hard negatives from
personalised-PageRank neighbourhoods with a curriculum~\cite{ying2018graph}. Our exposure result is the blended case on
a friend graph. Exposures added to random negatives still hurt, and the harm lies in pairing each exposure with its own
source, not in the added negatives or in who the exposed users are.

\head{Batch construction as negative mining} Composing batches so that in-batch negatives are hard is an established
alternative to mining them per example. TAS-Balanced clusters queries by topic and samples each batch from one
cluster~\cite{hofstatter2021tasb}. NGAME, in extreme classification, periodically clusters the training points on their
current embeddings into equal-size clusters. It builds each mini-batch from a few random clusters, so in-batch negatives
are hard without a mining index~\cite{dahiya2023ngame}. Our scheduler builds on this batch ordering. Two gaps remain.
At every refresh NGAME re-embeds all training points with the current encoder. And because its clusters are balanced in
data points, nothing bounds how many positive rows one cluster contributes to a batch. ANCE-Tele observes that
refreshing hard negatives makes dense retrievers forget, and counters it with negatives carried over from earlier
iterations~\cite{sun2022ancetele}. We add three things these works do not address. On a graph, embedding clusters are
graph regions, so clustered batches are also cheaper to encode. The clustering signal can be cached from training
itself instead of re-embedded. And batch concentration is a failure mode of its own, regional forgetting, which a
per-cluster cap prevents and a concentration statistic predicts.

\head{Graph locality and training efficiency} Sampling-based graph neural networks encode each node from a sampled
neighbourhood~\cite{hamilton2017inductive,ying2018graph}, so the cost of a batch depends on how much its neighbourhoods
overlap. Cluster-GCN exploits this by building batches from graph partitions~\cite{chiang2019clustergcn}.
Clustered-anchor batching obtains a similar locality without a partitioner, from the model's own embeddings. It applies
that locality to supervision rows rather than nodes. The regional forgetting we observe is an instance of catastrophic
interference under non-stationary training data~\cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}, here created by the batch scheduler.
