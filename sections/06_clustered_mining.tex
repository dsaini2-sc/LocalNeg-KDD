\section{Mining Negatives from Embedding Clusters}
\label{sec:method}

Section~\ref{sec:locality} shows that users who are close in embedding space are close in the graph. It also shows
that a user's graph neighbours look like positives to the model. \ours{} turns both facts into a training
signal. If a batch is built from a few clusters of similar users, every anchor's in-batch negatives come from its own
region, and the batch is cheaper to encode.

\subsection{Clustered-anchor batching}
\label{sec:method:batching}

Training proceeds in windows of 50 updates. At the start of a window, each worker takes the positive rows it will
train on in that window: 102{,}400 rows at 2{,}048 rows per batch. It clusters their distinct anchors by query
embedding into balanced clusters of about 64 anchors with recursive two-means. It then packs whole clusters, drawn in
random order, into batches, targeting 16 clusters per batch. All rows of an anchor stay in its cluster, so an anchor's
positives train together. Random negatives are kept (512 per worker, 8{,}192 per step), as is the frequency correction
of the loss. A per-cluster row cap bounds the rows any one cluster contributes to a batch (Section~\ref{sec:capping}).
The first 200 updates of every run use random batches. Clustering starts from that shared prefix model,
and every arm, random included, continues from the same prefix checkpoint.

\subsection{A cached clustering signal}
\label{sec:method:cache}

Clustering needs a current query embedding for every anchor of the next window. Computing them fresh means an
inference pass over the window's anchors every 50 updates, on the GPU and on the critical path. We instead keep a
cache of query embeddings that the training loop fills as a by-product. Every training step already computes the query
embeddings of its anchors, and we store them. A CPU thread clusters the next window from a snapshot of the cache while
the GPU trains on the current one. Gradients therefore always use current embeddings, and only the batch assignment
uses cached ones. A single inference pass with the prefix model fills the cache before the first clustered window.

Clustered batches are cheaper to encode (Section~\ref{sec:locality:cost}), so \ours{}'s training steps are 16\% faster
than random ones: an update takes 4.01\,s against 4.80\,s (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Clustering on a fresh signal
gives the saving back and more. Its steps take 4.10\,s, but its
inference pass costs 62\,s every 50 updates, 23\% of its training loop with the clustering, so it costs 11\% more GPU
time than random training. \ours{} exposes 43\,s of planning over the whole run, under 0.1\% of loop
time. We also charge it the one-time bootstrap pass of 5{,}459\,s. It still costs 9\% less GPU time than random
training, and 16\% less without the bootstrap. The bootstrap is 8\% of a three-epoch run and amortises with longer
training. Full-state checkpoints are larger, because the 2.7\,GB per-worker cache is part of the resumable state
(101\,s vs.\ 38\,s per save).

\subsection{Retrieval quality on the production graph}
\label{sec:method:results}

\ours{} improves retrieval at every point of training (Table~\ref{tab:internal}). After three epochs, random
training reaches 34.80\% macro recall@1000 and \ours{} 36.44\%. That is a gain of 1.6 points, or 4.7\% relative.
The gain is present from the first epoch (1.6 points) and does not close as training continues. The cached clustering
signal costs almost nothing in quality: a fresh signal differs from it by at most 0.28 points at any point. Capping, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:capping}, is neutral on this graph, within
0.26 points of the uncapped arm at every point.
